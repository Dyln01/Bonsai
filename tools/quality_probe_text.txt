The study of how rivers shape the land is older than most of the sciences that now describe it. A stream begins as a thin film of water running off a hillside after rain, and within minutes the film gathers into rivulets that cut small channels into the soil. Each channel carries away a little sediment, and the sediment it carries is the tool with which it cuts deeper. Over seasons the channels merge, the merged channels grow wider and straighter, and the slope of the whole valley slowly adjusts until the water neither gains nor loses much energy as it travels downhill. Engineers who build bridges and embankments learn early that a river is never finished. Even a channel that has looked the same for a human lifetime may be shifting its bends a few centimetres each flood, and a foundation placed on what seems to be solid ground can be undermined by water that has been working patiently for a century.

Bread offers a quieter lesson in patience. Flour and water, left alone together, begin to change on their own. Enzymes in the grain break starch into sugars, wild yeasts that arrived on the wheat or in the air feed on those sugars, and the bubbles they release are caught in a network of gluten that stretches without tearing. A baker does not so much create this process as guide it, choosing how warm the kitchen should be, how long the dough should rest, and when the loaf has risen far enough that the oven will finish the job. If the dough is baked too early it stays dense and heavy, and if it is baked too late the gluten gives way and the loaf collapses on itself. Experienced bakers say that the dough tells them when it is ready, which is another way of saying that they have watched enough loaves to recognise the moment by sight, by touch, and by smell.

Maps have always been arguments as much as pictures. Every mapmaker must decide what to leave out, since a chart that showed every stone and every footpath would be as large as the land it described and as useless to carry. The early navigators of the Mediterranean drew coastlines in careful detail and left the interior of the continents almost blank, because the sea was where their lives depended on accuracy. Later surveyors, working for governments that wanted to collect taxes and move armies, reversed the emphasis and filled the interior with roads, boundaries, and the outlines of fields. When satellites made it possible to photograph the whole planet from above, many people expected the art of map making to end, but it did not. A photograph still shows everything at once, and a good map is valuable precisely because it chooses a single thing worth seeing and makes it clear.

The printing press changed the world less by making books cheaper than by making them identical. Before print, every copy of a text was written out by hand, and every copyist introduced small errors that the next copyist then copied faithfully along with the rest. Scholars who wished to compare two manuscripts of the same work often found that they differed in hundreds of places, and deciding which version was correct could occupy a lifetime. Once a page could be set in metal type and printed a thousand times, a reader in one city could be certain that a reader in another city was looking at exactly the same words. Arguments about what an author had really said could finally be settled by pointing to a page, and a new kind of shared knowledge became possible, in which people who had never met could work from the same facts.

Weather forecasting shows how a problem can become easier and harder at the same time. The equations that govern the motion of the atmosphere have been known for well over a century, and modern computers can solve them for the whole globe several times a day. Yet the atmosphere is a system in which tiny differences grow, so that a small error in the measured temperature over an ocean on Monday can become a wrong prediction about a storm on Saturday. Forecasters respond by running the same calculation many times with slightly different starting values and watching how far the results spread apart. When the runs agree, they speak with confidence, and when the runs scatter, they say honestly that the future is uncertain, and that admission is itself a useful piece of information for anyone deciding whether to carry an umbrella.
